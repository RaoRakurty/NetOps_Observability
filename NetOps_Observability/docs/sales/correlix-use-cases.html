The gap
01

What today's observability platforms miss

Modern networks are not under-monitored. They are over-notified. Traditional platforms collect everything and conclude nothing — the hardest work, turning signals into a cause, is left to the engineer at 2 a.m. Three gaps do the damage.

Gap A

Alert storms without causality

One fault produces two hundred notifications, each technically true, none of them the answer. Dashboards show what is red; nothing says why. The correlation happens in an engineer's head, under pressure, one console at a time.

Gap B

Black-box AI, unverifiable RCA

“AIOps” tools issue a verdict and hide the reasoning. A NOC cannot escalate, page a vendor, or wake an executive on an unverifiable claim — so engineers re-verify everything by hand, and the “automation” saves nothing.

Gap C

No responsibility seam

The most expensive question in networking — is it us, or the carrier? — has no owner. Tools stop measuring at the demarc, the provider says “no problem on our side,” and both parties burn a night proving a negative.

The idea
02

Correlate. Corroborate. Prove.

Correlix ingests every telemetry plane into one correlation engine. The engine matches event patterns against built-in network fault-family signatures and statistical baselines, then does the thing no dashboard does: it corroborates independent streams into one ranked incident — root cause named, owner recommended, evidence attached — and files the ticket itself.

The discipline that makes it trustworthy

Correlix marks an incident CONFIRMED only when at least two independent telemetry streams agree — a control-plane event and a data-path measurement, degrading in the same activity window. Anything less is honestly labeled SUSPECTED, with the missing evidence listed. Your NOC always knows exactly how much to trust a verdict — because the verdict shows its work.

Use case 1 · The provider seam
03

“Is it us, or the carrier?”

A WAN circuit degrades at night. Two independent streams answer the question — with proof.

The scenario

An enterprise runs its primary internet circuit through a national carrier. On a quiet Tuesday night, the circuit begins to degrade — not down, just wrong. The BGP session to the provider turns unstable, and latency that users feel climbs with it. Nightly replication crawls; the remote workforce's VPN sessions stutter.

  • 22:04 Round-trip time on the circuit starts climbing — 24 ms → 180 ms over ten minutes, with intermittent packet loss.
  • 22:09 The BGP session to the provider edge flaps. Then again. And again.
  • 22:15 Alert queues fill: interface warnings, reachability checks, application slowness — 47 alerts across 9 devices.
Today's tools

The NPM shows every interface up and green — technically true, operationally useless. The synthetic-monitoring tool sees the latency but can't say why; the routing logs sit in a third console nobody has open. By 23:30 there's a war room: five engineers, three tools, one whiteboard. The carrier is called, and answers with the sentence every network engineer knows by heart: “We see no problem on our side.” Without proof, the night becomes an exercise in proving a negative.

With Correlix

Correlix has two independent views of the same circuit: control-plane events from the edge router (the BGP session state), and an active path probe continuously measuring the customer path itself. At 22:09 both begin degrading — in the same activity window. The engine corroborates them, promotes the incident to CONFIRMED, and names the fault domain: internet egress, provider side of the seam.

The recommended owner is the carrier. The RCA Auto-Ticketing policy files one ticket — not forty-seven — carrying the evidence bundle: both streams, the activity window, the fault-domain determination. The escalation to the ISP opens with proof, not opinion. “We see no problem on our side” stops being the end of the conversation.

Use case 2 · The alert-storm cascade
04

200 alerts, one failing uplink

A degrading optic takes three floors with it. Correlix separates the cause from 213 symptoms.

The scenario

Tuesday, 09:12, the worst possible time: the campus is full. On a distribution switch, the optic on uplink Te1/0/48 begins to fail — not dead, flapping. Each flap tears down the OSPF adjacency riding the link; each recovery rebuilds it. Downstream, access switches and access points fall intermittently unreachable, and every one of them reports it. Syslog bursts from everywhere at once.

  • 09:12 Uplink Te1/0/48 starts flapping — 22 state transitions in eleven minutes.
  • 09:13 OSPF adjacencies across the link flap in lock-step. Reconvergence ripples through the distribution layer.
  • 09:23 214 device-level alerts across switches, APs, and reachability monitors — every one marked critical.
Today's tools

Every traditional console lights up with the same storm, and every alert claims equal urgency: the unreachable AP on floor 3 looks exactly as important as the flapping uplink that took it down. The actual cause is alert #147 of 214 — a one-line interface warning buried between two hundred consequences. Triage runs on seniority and instinct. The engineer who happens to remember “check the distribution uplinks first” is the monitoring strategy.

With Correlix

The engine recognizes the shape of the storm. The event pattern matches two built-in fault-family signatures — link-flap and routing-adjacency-flap — and the topology says everything alarming sits downstream of one interface. Correlix separates cause from symptom: the causal graph pins Te1/0/48 as the root, and the other 213 alerts are grouped under it as impact — evidence of blast radius, not competing emergencies.

Execution
05

Why this works

Both use cases run on the same four design decisions. They are the product.

Every plane, one platform

Metrics, flows, logs, traps, and active path probes land in the same correlation engine with a shared clock and a shared topology. No tool-hopping, no reconstructing a timeline across three consoles — the cross-plane comparison that solves incidents happens automatically.

Evidence, or it didn't happen

Every conclusion carries its evidence log: click any claim in an incident and see the raw signals behind it. Confirmed requires two independent streams; Suspected says so out loud and lists what's missing. There is no black box to take on faith.

The responsibility seam, built in

Fault domains are modeled on both sides of the demarc — inside your network, or on the provider's side of the seam. When the evidence points outward, you escalate to the carrier with proof attached, and the “no problem on our side” conversation changes shape.

Closes the loop

RCA Auto-Ticketing files one ticket per root cause into ServiceNow or Jira — evidence bundle attached — and incident time decomposition shows where the minutes actually went: time to detect, to correlate, to isolate the root, to assign the owner. You manage what you can finally measure.

And when you'd rather just ask: “What's going on right now?” — Correlix AI answers in plain language from your live network data, every statement cited back to the evidence it came from.
